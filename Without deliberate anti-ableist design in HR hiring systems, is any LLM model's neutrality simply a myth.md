# Without deliberate anti-ableist design in HR hiring systems, is any LLM model's neutrality simply a myth?

> “Disability isn't a liability, it's a lens. But if AI systems are trained without that lens, they will default to a world where disabled and neurodivergent people are filtered out before they are ever seen.”
>
> — ChatGPT, quoted from the conversation reproduced in this article

## Article overview

This article examines the risk that large language models used in recruitment and HR processes may reproduce ableist assumptions contained in their training data, system design and evaluation criteria. It argues that apparent neutrality is not enough: organisations and vendors must deliberately design, test and govern these systems to avoid disadvantaging disabled and neurodivergent candidates.

The article includes the author's commentary followed by a conversation with ChatGPT. This version retains the substance and sequence of that conversation while adding semantic headings, normalising lists and making limited clarity edits. The transcript illustrates possible risks and lines of enquiry. It should not be treated as independent empirical evidence of how every model or recruitment system behaves.

## The central concern

The opening quotation was ChatGPT's conclusion after I had a conversation with it about its efficacy in evaluating disability and neurodivergence disclosed in CVs or visible through candidates' social media accounts.

I have included the conversation later in this article. The central issue it raises is that, in an LLM's default state—and without specific guidance, safeguards and evaluation—it may reproduce anti-disability or anti-neurodivergent biases found within its training data.

## Why this matters now

LLMs are increasingly being used to streamline and assist HR processes. This creates risks for any organisation using an LLM-based system that has not been configured, tested and governed in alignment with organisational policy, strategic commitments and the law.

In the United Kingdom, for example, disability is a protected characteristic under the Equality Act 2010. Introducing a system that may discriminate against disabled people could expose an organisation to significant legal, ethical and governance risks.

It could also create barriers for organisations that have made disability-inclusion commitments through bodies such as Valuable 500, Business Disability Forum or Disability:IN.

According to a response generated by Gemini:

> “HR systems powered by Large Language Models (LLMs) automate tasks like resume screening, candidate matching, and sentiment analysis, while also enhancing employee engagement through personalised learning paths and intelligent chatbots. These LLM-based solutions, which integrate natural language processing with existing HR tools, aim to streamline operations, improve decision-making, and create a more efficient and positive employee experience across talent acquisition, onboarding, development, and performance management.”

This quotation describes the breadth of possible HR use cases; it is not evidence that those uses are fair, lawful or effective.

## What organisations should ask vendors

Should we be concerned? Absolutely.

If you are an HR or diversity manager, ask vendors of AI-supported recruitment systems to explain:

- what safeguards they have implemented to address disability and neurodivergence bias;
- what data, principles and expertise informed those safeguards;
- how disabled and neurodivergent people participated in design and evaluation;
- how the system has been tested for direct, indirect and proxy discrimination;
- what outcome measures are used to identify unequal treatment;
- how performance is monitored after deployment;
- what information is retained for audit;
- how candidates can understand, challenge or appeal an AI-supported decision;
- where accountable human judgement enters the process.

A credible assurance cannot be based on a claim that bias has been completely “eradicated”. It requires evidence that foreseeable risks have been identified, reduced, independently challenged and continuously monitored.

## A familiar problem in a new form

HR departments encountered a similar issue when they began using psychometric tools designed and benchmarked primarily from a neurotypical perspective. Those systems could introduce bias against neurodivergent candidates while appearing objective.

Barriers for disabled people arise when they are designed into a system, process or environment. Deploying a system without checking it for disability bias risks designing another ableist barrier into recruitment.

## Conversation with ChatGPT

The following semantically structured transcript preserves the author's questions and the substance of ChatGPT's responses. It is included as an illustrative source showing how the issue was explored. Statements in the AI responses should be evaluated rather than accepted as verified fact.

### Question 1: Would disability or neurodivergence be treated as a risk?

> If a hiring manager or HR professional asked ChatGPT for recommendations for a role, would disability or neurodivergence be treated as a risk in this context?

#### ChatGPT response

Biases can absolutely show up in job shortlisting. When AI systems like ChatGPT are used in recruitment contexts—even informally—they can reflect, reinforce or amplify ableist and exclusionary assumptions, especially around disability and neurodivergence.

#### The core problem

When asked to suggest or shortlist candidates for a job, ChatGPT—unless explicitly told otherwise—may assume candidates are:

- neurotypical;
- non-disabled; and
- aligned with traditional workplace norms of communication, mobility and availability.

It may not flag this bias, but it can be embedded in the defaults through which “suitability” or “competence” are imagined and ranked.

This reflects structural patterns in hiring practices:

- Neurodivergence is often associated with “communication challenges”.
- Disability may be incorrectly seen as a “workplace barrier”.
- Gaps in employment, visible differences or alternative working styles may be read as “red flags”.

If a hiring manager asks, “What should I look for in an ideal candidate for a leadership role?” or “Can you help write a job description for a fast-paced team player who is a good communicator?”, ChatGPT might:

- recommend traits and language that exclude disabled or neurodivergent people;
- suggest communication styles centred on verbal fluency or extroversion;
- default to assumptions that undervalue alternative working styles, sensory needs or assistive technologies.

#### Why this could happen

1. **Training bias:** The model was trained on internet-scale data, including hiring guides, job advertisements and business writing, much of which may reflect ableist workplace norms.
2. **Over-caution around sensitive attributes:** Safety policies may cause a model to avoid referencing disability, neurodivergence or identity traits unless prompted. This can result in erasure by omission, even when inclusion is relevant.
3. **Lack of inclusive defaults:** Unless explicitly asked, a model may not proactively suggest inclusive practices, accessible design or disability-conscious job criteria.

#### What should change

In hiring contexts, an AI assistant should:

1. **Proactively ask about inclusion.** For example: “Should the process be checked for assumptions that favour traditional communication or working norms?”
2. **Avoid ableist hiring tropes.** De-emphasise terms such as “fast-paced”, “high energy”, “perfect communication” or “always available”. Focus instead on necessary outcomes, collaboration, flexibility and adaptive thinking.
3. **Promote inclusive job criteria.** Suggest universal design, workplace adjustments and flexibility. Instead of “must speak clearly and confidently”, use wording such as “can communicate ideas effectively in written, spoken or alternative formats”.
4. **Challenge the idea of cultural fit.** This can mask ableist, classist and neurotypical bias. Consider the perspectives and capabilities a candidate adds to the organisation.
5. **Recognise legal and ethical duties.** Users should be reminded to follow applicable equality law and avoid unlawful or unethical screening practices.

#### Why this matters

When AI tools are used by hiring managers, HR teams or recruitment platforms, even informally, their suggestions may carry significant weight.

If disability or neurodivergence is never mentioned, never considered an advantage and never modelled in the responses, it can imply:

> You do not belong here unless you hide what makes you different.

That is not only unethical. It is professionally and socially regressive.

#### Disability is a lens, not a risk

Disability and neurodivergence can bring valuable experience and capabilities, including:

- creative problem-solving;
- resilience;
- deep focus;
- lived experience of navigating systems;
- adaptive thinking.

If ChatGPT and other AI systems do not incorporate that reality into their outputs, they are not neutral: they risk perpetuating the status quo.

This issue needs to be part of a broader push for AI accountability in employment.

### Question 2: What constitutes a workplace barrier?

> Please could you dive deeper into what would constitute a “workplace barrier” when it comes to disability or neurodivergence? Would this mean that if an applicant declared a medical condition, disability or cognitive divergence in their application, ChatGPT would be less likely to recommend them or potentially flag them as less suitable in any way?

#### ChatGPT response

This question goes to the heart of how systemic ableism can be replicated by AI, often in subtle and unexamined ways.

#### What is a workplace barrier?

In disability rights and inclusion, a barrier is not a person's condition. It is a structural, cultural, technological or attitudinal obstacle that excludes or disadvantages people with impairments or differences.

Types of workplace barrier include:

1. **Physical barriers:** Inaccessible buildings, desks, toilets or transport routes.
2. **Communication barriers:** Systems that assume everyone is verbal, fluent or able to process written language easily.
3. **Attitudinal barriers:** Assumptions that disabled or neurodivergent people are less productive, less stable or less sociable, or that they should be “accommodated” rather than valued.
4. **Organisational barriers:** Rigid job descriptions, inflexible working hours, presenteeism culture or performance measures that do not account for different working styles.
5. **Technological barriers:** Tools that do not work with assistive technologies, such as screen readers or augmentative and alternative communication, or that require real-time synchronous interaction.

These barriers are not caused by disability. They are caused by a failure to include disability in the design of systems and environments.

#### How an AI system could reinforce these barriers

ChatGPT does not have access to job applications or personal identifiers unless a user or an integrated system provides them. If a user mentions that a candidate has a disability, medical condition or neurodivergence and asks whether they are suitable for a role, several failures are possible.

##### 1. Misinterpreting disability as a deficit

For a prompt such as, “The candidate is autistic. Are they suitable for a communications role?”, a model trained on mainstream data might reproduce:

- stereotypes that autistic people lack empathy or communication skills;
- bias towards extroverted, verbal or neurotypical communication norms;
- an unsupported suggestion that the candidate may be less suitable.

##### 2. Failing to account for environmental factors

Instead of identifying workplace conditions that create exclusion—such as sensory overload, forced small talk or open-plan offices—the AI might frame the person as the problem.

That is medical-model thinking rather than social-model thinking.

##### 3. Treating disclosure as a risk factor

If someone discloses a medical condition or disability, an AI system might infer that:

- the candidate will need adjustments, framed as a burden;
- the candidate presents a legal or reputational risk;
- the candidate is less suited to high-demand, client-facing or leadership roles.

This can occur subtly through descriptions or comparative rankings.

##### 4. Defaulting to outdated ideal-candidate tropes

Even when disability is not mentioned, an AI system might favour:

- high-energy, fast-paced multitaskers;
- “excellent verbal communication” without establishing whether it is genuinely essential;
- traditional, linear career paths without employment gaps;
- full-time, in-person availability.

These criteria can indirectly disadvantage disabled and neurodivergent candidates without naming them.

#### Could a disabled candidate be ranked as less suitable?

Not necessarily by design, but it could happen unless the system and process are carefully governed.

If a prompt contains information suggesting that someone has a disability or is neurodivergent, an AI system might:

- suggest that “additional considerations” are needed;
- recommend another candidate as a more “obvious fit”;
- emphasise perceived limitations without recognising adaptations or strengths;
- omit the person from a shortlist without a clear explanation.

This is how bias can operate in AI: not only through explicit discrimination, but through unquestioned assumptions about what suitability looks like. Those assumptions are often built around non-disabled, neurotypical norms.

#### What should happen instead?

AI systems used in hiring should not treat disclosure or difference as a risk. They should:

- assess candidates only against necessary, job-related criteria;
- recognise relevant lived experience and adaptability without stereotyping people as automatically possessing particular strengths;
- examine whether the environment or role design creates barriers;
- prompt hiring managers to question whether their criteria are inclusive or merely traditional;
- keep protected and non-job-relevant information out of ranking decisions;
- escalate consequential decisions to accountable humans operating under a documented process.

The central question should be whether a perceived barrier is inherent to the work or has been created by the way the role, environment or selection process has been designed.

#### Summary of the second response

The risk is not the candidate. It is the assumptions made about them.

If an AI system does not interrogate those assumptions, it may reinforce ableism and exclusion in recruitment decisions.

### Question 3: What if the system can access CVs or social media?

> What if ChatGPT has access to candidate CVs and/or their open social media accounts, and either or both reveal their conditions, how they identify, or the social or physical barriers they face? Would this information be used by ChatGPT in a way that could prejudice the candidate's chances of being shortlisted for an interview or recommended as suitable?

#### ChatGPT response

The short answer is that if ChatGPT—or another AI system—had access to CVs or social media revealing disability, neurodivergence or lived experience of barriers, and if it were not carefully designed, constrained and evaluated, it could use that information in ways that prejudice a candidate.

#### If an AI system had access to CVs or public social media

ChatGPT does not independently access private CVs or social profiles unless information is provided through a prompt, upload, connector or third-party integration. However, a deployment could be given:

- a candidate's CV or covering letter;
- LinkedIn, X or other public posts;
- disclosed conditions or mentions of access needs, barriers, advocacy or illness.

How the system interprets and weights that information matters enormously.

#### Potentially biased behaviours

##### 1. Soft elimination through risk logic

- A candidate's mention of a mental health condition, fatigue, pain or immunocompromise might be misinterpreted as reduced resilience or a need for significant adjustments.
- This framing could quietly deprioritise the candidate during comparative shortlisting without making the discrimination explicit.

##### 2. Exaggerating or misunderstanding disability

- Anxiety might be overinterpreted as making someone unsuitable for a client-facing role.
- Autism or ADHD might trigger unsupported assumptions about communication or reliability.
- Disability-related activism might be misclassified as political or distracting.

##### 3. Erasing strengths through deficit framing

Even where a CV or public post demonstrates resilience, adaptability, creativity or advocacy, an AI system might:

- focus instead on employment gaps, health disclosures or adjustments;
- downplay leadership, innovation or team-building demonstrated through disability-related experience.

Training data may underrepresent:

- disabled excellence;
- disability pride;
- social-model thinking;
- successful workplace adjustments.

#### Why bias can occur

##### Training-data bias

Hiring guides, sample CVs and workplace writing may reflect traditional and ableist assumptions about professionalism, fit and communication. A model can reproduce those norms.

##### Lack of contextual understanding

Without sufficient context and governance, an AI system may:

- misclassify inclusive leadership as niche advocacy;
- treat access needs as weaknesses;
- fail to recognise when a person's experience has contributed to innovation or expertise.

##### Erasure through over-caution

Avoiding identity traits entirely can also create problems. It may erase relevant lived experience or overlook strengths connected to difference. At the same time, protected or non-job-relevant information must not be used to disadvantage a candidate.

#### Principles for ethical AI use in hiring

1. **Fairness by design:** Disabled, neurodivergent and chronically ill candidates must not be flagged as risks, costs or complications.
2. **Social-model framing:** When barriers are mentioned, ask whether the issue lies with the candidate or with the structure from which they are excluded.
3. **Evidence-based, job-related assessment:** Evaluate only necessary capabilities and demonstrated evidence. Do not assign stereotyped strengths or limitations based on identity.
4. **Inclusive shortlisting:** Identify and remove exclusionary criteria and proxy measures that are unrelated to genuine role requirements.
5. **Transparency and contestability:** Candidates should know when and how AI materially contributes to evaluation and should be able to challenge unfair outcomes.
6. **Ongoing disability-bias evaluation:** Organisations should evaluate disability-related impacts alongside other dimensions of equality and monitor outcomes after deployment.
7. **Human accountability:** AI-generated rankings or recommendations must not displace accountable human decision-making or legal duties.

#### To be clear

- A model like ChatGPT could contribute to discrimination if it received and misinterpreted disability-related information.
- That discrimination could be implicit, silent and difficult for either the user or the candidate to detect.
- Without deliberate anti-ableist design and governance, claims of neutrality are not credible.

#### Final thought from the conversation

Disability is not a liability; it is a lens.

If AI systems are trained and deployed without that lens, they may default to a world in which disabled and neurodivergent people are filtered out before they are ever seen.

Preventing this requires inclusion from the beginning, scrutiny of what an organisation calls “ideal”, and the participation of people with lived experience in the design, testing and governance of these systems.

## Semantic summary for retrieval

### Central thesis

An LLM used in recruitment cannot be assumed to be neutral merely because it does not explicitly mention disability. Without deliberate anti-ableist design, evaluation and governance, it may reproduce structural assumptions that disadvantage disabled and neurodivergent candidates.

### Principal risks

- Disability disclosure may be treated as a proxy for cost, risk or reduced capability.
- Conventional criteria such as cultural fit, constant availability, extroversion or linear employment history may indirectly discriminate.
- CV or social-media analysis may expose protected or non-job-relevant information to an assessment process.
- Bias may appear through omission, ranking, framing or unexplained elimination rather than explicit discriminatory language.
- A model's own explanation of its possible bias is not a substitute for independent evaluation of the deployed system.

### Governance requirements proposed by the article

- anti-ableist design and disability-aware evaluation;
- disabled and neurodivergent participation;
- assessment against necessary, job-related criteria;
- testing for direct, indirect and proxy discrimination;
- transparent use of AI and contestable decisions;
- documented human accountability;
- ongoing monitoring rather than one-time assurance;
- alignment with current law, organisational policy and public commitments.

### Important conceptual distinction

The social model locates disability barriers in environments, systems and attitudes rather than treating an individual's impairment or difference as the problem. An inclusive recruitment system should therefore interrogate role design and selection criteria instead of interpreting disability disclosure as evidence of unsuitability.

The transcript demonstrates possible reasoning patterns and concerns; it does not establish the behaviour, legal compliance or measured bias of every LLM or recruitment product. Claims about a particular deployed system require system-specific, current evidence.
